Cartilage conduction as the third pathway for sound transmission

1. Introduction
It has long been recognized that air conduction (AC) and
bone conduction (BC) are two major mediators for sound
conduction to the inner ear. In the AC pathway, the eardrum is
stimulated by air vibration through the external ear canal,
whereas BC is mediated mainly by bone vibration based on
sound signals. In 2004, Hosoi found that vibration of aural
cartilage, generated by placing gently a transducer on the
cartilage, could create clear audible sound [1,2]. His findings,
which were described in the patent documents, are as follows;
when the vibration, including speech information, is delivered
to the aural cartilage, the speech information can reach the
inner ear with the same clarity as that from AC and BC. It is a
different sound transmission from the well-known AC and BC.
The tragus is one of the most effective places to put a
transducer. The loudness of sound changes according to the
contact pressure between the tragus and the transducer, which
is a different property from AC and BC. Hosoi also proposed
using his findings in novel communication devices such as
hearing aids, telephones, communication devices, etc. Thus, he
proposed the concept (and term) of “cartilage conduction
(CC)” sound, which is difficult to categorize within the two
previously known types of conduction. These findings
identified CC as a third mediator for delivering sound to
the inner ear.

A B S T R A C T
It has been long considered that air and bone are the two major mediators that conduct sounds to the
inner ear. In 2004, Hosoi found that vibration of aural cartilage, generated by placing gently a
transducer on it, could create audible sound with the same level of clarity as air- and bone-
conduction sound. He thus proposed the term “cartilage conduction” for this concept. This research
identified a third mediator for sound conduction to the inner ear. Hosoi also proposed the
development of novel communication devices, such as hearing aids, telephones, etc. using his
findings.
For cartilage conduction, three sound pathways can be assumed. The transducer vibration may
cause airborne sound which passes into the external auditory canal through the canal entrance (direct
air pathway). Alternatively, the vibration at the cartilage may generate audible sound in the external
auditory canal (cartilage-air pathway), or propagate directly to the inner ear through the skull bone
(cartilage-bone pathway). A series of studies has illustrated that the cartilage-air pathway is
dominant for hearing sensations in listeners with normal ears. The cartilage-bone pathway works for
patients with bony aural atresia. A fourth pathway, the fibrotic-tissue pathway, is considered to act in
the case of fibrotic aural atresia.
In this review, we summarize this series of studies and discuss the nature of cartilage conduction.

The aural cartilage is distributed around the exterior half of
the external auditory canal. The cartilage and transducer play
the roles of a diaphragm and voice coil of a loudspeaker,
respectively, in normal ears. In contrast to the auditory
properties of dilatational waves that are externally generated in
the air, CC produces sound directly in the external auditory
canal. Unlike that for the heavy bone of the skull, conduction
through the light aural cartilage requires minimal force, and
thus likely could be driven by a powerless transducer (Fig. 1).
Regarding BC, bone is probably not the only material for
sound transmission, and, instead, other tissue surrounding the
bone may also contribute to the conduction of vibration in this
pathway. Stenfelt and Goode [3] tested whether other parts
beyond cranial bone contribute to BC. They found that several
processes, including middle-ear ossicle inertia, inertia of the
cochlear fluids, compression of the cochlear walls, and pressure
transmission from cerebrospinal fluid can be involved in the
process of bone conduction. Likewise, other investigators have
demonstrated that soft cranial tissues, such as the fontanel, eye,
cheek and neck, contribute to the production of auditory
sensations [4–6]. These data indicate that several tissues other
than bone can contribute to BC, that is, that there can be non-
osseous bone conduction. However, the significant difference
between these non-osseous BCs and CC is the sound energy
reaching the cochlea. Non-osseous BC sounds cannot be heard
like AC or BC sounds, but CC sound can be heard as well as AC
or BC sound. Prior to the studies of non-osseous BC just
described, several scholars closely investigated the physical
routes for BC [7,8]. They found that sound mediated by BC
could be radiated into the ear canal. In fact, Berthold, in
Germany, is believed to have been one of the first people who
objectively demonstrated the air pressure in the auditory canal
by using microphonics [9]. Later, Khanna et al. [8] also showed
that low-frequency sound in BC was transmitted to the external
ear canal prior to reaching the inner ear. These data suggested
that BC could not be caused by bone vibration alone, and that
the presence of sound at the external ear canal is also important.
However, it remained unclear how bone vibration gave rise to
sound at the external ear canal.
The external auditory canal has a unique characteristic, being
composed of two different materials. One material is cartilage,
in the outer part, whereas the other is bone, in the inner part.
Recently, Stenfelt et al. [10] attempted to determine the role of
cartilage by removing cartilage along with soft tissue from the
canal. They measured sound pressure levels (SPL) at the canal
in BC and found that removing the cartilage resulted in a
reduction in the SPL at the ear canal of approximately 10 dB
under BC stimulation. Those data clearly demonstrated that the
cartilaginous part of the ear canal could contribute to producing
sound at the external canal. General studies of BC have been
performed by vibrating the bone whereas CC works by
vibrating the cartilage.
After Hosoi discovered CC, we developed a piezoelectric
bimorph type of transducer with a ring-shaped attachment
dedicated to effectively vibrating the aural cartilage [11]. Since
then, we have attempted to identify the specific characteristics of
CC using the new transducer [11–23]. As the result of our studies,
we have proposed that CC could be a third independent pathway.
We then demonstrated the utility of CC hearing aids, using a
newly developed balanced-armature type of transducer [24]. In
this review, we introduce our series of studies and discuss CC.

3. Suitable transducer location and importance of the
cartilage component for CC
In order to identify the physical routes of CC, we first
determined where to put the transducer to maximize CC hearing
[13,14,18]. Nishimura et al. [18] approached the problem with a
psycho-acoustic procedure. Eight volunteers (4 females and
4 males; 28–37 years old) with normal hearing participated in
this experiment. The transducer was located on the tragus (aural
cartilage), pretragus (soft tissue), and mastoid (bone), and the
participants’ hearing thresholds for frequencies of 0.5, 1, 2, and
4 kHz were measured. To minimize the effect of AC sound from
the transducer, the external auditory canal of each participant
was occluded with an earplug. At 0.5 kHz, the threshold for the
tragus condition was 22.1 dB lower than that for the pretragus
condition and 29.1 dB lower than that for the mastoid condition.
At 1 kHz, the threshold differences were smaller, at 15.1 dB and
22.2 dB, respectively, and at 2 kHz the differences were even
smaller, at 13.8 dB and 12.3 dB, respectively. At 4 kHz, the
thresholds for the tragus, pretragus and mastoid conditions did
not differ significantly. As long as the output vibration was
lower than 4 kHz, CC contributed to hearing during stimulation
of the tragus.
In another study, Shimokura et al. [14] reported the
differences in physical measurements for different transducer
locations. After a transducer was placed at the entrance of the
ear canal, behind the concha, on the ear lobe or on the zygomatic
arch as shown in Fig. 3, vibration acceleration levels (VALs)
were measured at three points (ear tragus, scaphoid fossa, and
concha) in and around the auricle. A 37-year-old man who had
no disorders of the outer ear participated. Fig. 4a shows the
averaged VALs in the three measurement points according to
the four stimulation points. It was found that the transmitted
vibration was stronger at the entrance of the ear canal and
behind the concha for the low-frequency range (<3 kHz). These
measurement results are consistent with the hearing-threshold
results reported by Nishimura et al. [18]. Given the fact that
these body parts contain substantial cartilage, vibration could
well be transmitted through cartilage in the outer ear. In the
physical measurement, the sound amplification by the CC was
measured by inserting a probe microphone into the external
auditory canal (Fig. 4b). The amplification level was measured
by calculating the difference in SPL when the transducer
touched the stimulation points, i.e. all three pathways of CC
versus when it was separated from them by a few millimeters,
i.e. only direct air pathway.
The results suggest that vibrations applied at positions with a
cartilaginous component (specifically, at the entrance of the canal
and behind the concha) were effectively transmitted to the entire
aural cartilage and generated more sound in the external auditory
canal. The results of these two experiments show the importance
of the cartilaginous component in the CC sound pathway.

Results showed that the loudness for CC was identical to the

stimulation. In contrast, the loudness for BC was much higher
than the SPL in the external auditory canal for bone stimulation.
These data suggest that the sound through the cartilage-air

loudness for CC would not be identical to the SPL if the
cartilage-bone pathway was dominant. Hence, the dominant
pathway in CC in normal ears is not the direct air pathway, nor
the cartilage-bone pathway, but the cartilage-air pathway.
5. Cartilage-air pathway for CC
Although CC is similar to BC in terms of touching with a
vibrator, there are some basic differences, such as the location
where a transducer is fixed and the conduction process from the
sound origin to the inner ear. In CC, the vibrated aural cartilage
generates sound in theexternal auditory canal, as confirmed in our
previous work [16,18]. Shimokura et al. [15] focused on the
cartilage-air pathway and examined the relationship between the
SPL in the canal and the application force at the aural cartilage,
because CC hearing becomes louder when the transducer pushes
more strongly at the ear tragus. In this study, a new transducer,
which has a smaller contact surface than the ring-shaped
transducer, was touched to the ear tragus. The ear tragus remained
at its initial position below an applied force of 1 N, and moved
toward the eardrum with forces greater than 1 N. After all, higher
pressure caused the ear tragus to occlude the external auditory
canal. In this experiment, we tested how the amount of application
force (from 0 to 3 N) would alter the SPL in the canal. In the force
range below 1 N, the increase in SPL was most apparent below
2 kHz (Fig. 5). In the range above 1 N, when the ear tragus started
to occlude the external auditory canal, the SPL below 1 kHz was
amplified. This finding regarding the relationship between
occlusion and the amplification of SPL in CC appears consistent
with a report from Stenfelt’s research group showing that
frequencies below 1 kHz are amplified by the occlusion of the
canal in bone stimulation [10].
6. Applications of CC
6.1. CC hearing aid
We have developed a CC hearing aid (Fig. 6) for patients
who cannot use conventional AC hearing aids (due, for
example, to atresia of the external auditory canal or severe
otorrhea) [11–13,24]. Although such patients are usually
encouraged to use a BC hearing aid, BC hearing aids require
strong pressure against the cranial bone, hence many patients
suffer from pain and annoyance. In addition, the sustained
pressure on the skull causes several disorders at the contact
point on the skin, including a cave-in and erosion. Furthermore,
the transmission efficacy of acoustic information is low,
therefore considerably large energy is needed to transmit
vibrations to the heavy skull bone. In contrast, CC does not
require strong and sustained pressure on the user’s tissues,
because cartilage is much lighter than skull bone so less
vibrational power is needed. Thus, cartilage could be a high-
performance converter. In fact, we found that the thresholds for
CC were lower than those for BC below 1 kHz, suggesting that
sound in the low- and middle-frequency range is transmitted
more efficiently by CC. Thus hearing aids for CC would
provide great benefit to patients with outer ear disorders.
Wefirstfocused on patientssufferingfrom atresia with fibrotic
tissue [17]. In that study, we proposed the existence of a fourth
pathway for CC, i.e. a fibrotic-tissue pathway, in addition to the
three possible pathways described above. The CC hearing aid is
also useful for patients with aural bony atresia; in that case, the
cartilage-bone pathway would be the dominant pathway [24].

6.3. Possible CC applications in the future
CC has the potential to be used in new products that are hard
to realize with conventional AC and BC [27].
6.3.1. Eyeglass-type communication device (mobile phones,
computer terminals, hearing aids, etc.)
Vibration from a vibrator on the temples in a pair of glasses
could be transmitted from behind the auricle to the cartilage of
the ear canal to produce sound in the canal. Users of this device
could hear sound without putting earphones in their ears, as
shown in Fig. 8(a).
6.3.2. Wristwatch-type communication device (mobile phones,
computer terminals, etc.)
A vibrator in a wristwatch or wristband would cause vibrations
at the fingertip. The vibrations would then be transmitted from the
finger to the ear canal cartilage, where the vibration would
produce sound in the canal, as shown in Fig. 8(b).
6.3.3. Pen-type communication device (mobile phones,
computer terminals, etc.)
CC could bring very slim mobile phones into reality, as
shown in Fig. 8(c). This type would be very convenient when
used with a tablet PC.

7. Standardization of CC
In anticipation of the popularization of CC devices, we are
researching a measurement method to standardize the output
sound from the devices [19]. For example, sound pressure
provided by conventional AC hearing aids is standardized with
an ear simulator [28] embedded in a head and torso simulator
(HATS) [29]. However, CC simulation by the HATS does not
fit the spectral curve of SPL in a human ear, because the
conduction structures differ between AC and CC [19]. Our
proposed calibration method will determine practically the
perceived sound passing through the CC.
8. Proposed new classification for sound conduction
pathways for normal external auditory canal
A series of our experiments demonstrated that CC is a unique
pathway, independent of AC and BC, so we proposed a new
classification for sound conduction pathways in normal ears
[22,23], as summarized in Table 1.
1) Air conduction pathway: Sound information in the form of
air vibration emitted from a sound source outside of the ear
enters the external auditory canal through its opening and
passes through the middle-ear sound conduction system to
reach the inner ear.
2) Bone conduction pathway: Sound information in the form
of vibration is input to the skull, and then the vibration is
conducted through the temporal bone to reach the inner ear.
3) Cartilage conduction pathway: Sound information in the
form of vibration is input to aural cartilage, thereby
generating airborne sound in the external auditory canal,

We now provide some additional details on the classification.
In the beginning of our study, we hypothesized three possible
sound conduction pathways in the ears of the normal external
auditory canal, i.e. the direct air pathway, the cartilage-air
pathway and the cartilage-bone pathway. Actually, the direct air
pathway is identical to the widely known normal AC pathway and
the cartilage-bone pathway is the well-known normal BC
pathway, even if there is a difference regarding whether the
cartilage exists, or not, in the pathway. The cartilage-air pathway
cannot be classified into either AC or BC, and this pathway is
identified as the main pathway of CC in normal ears.
Although all sounds reach the cochlea via the above-
described three sound conduction pathways in the natural
world, audio devices were generally classified into AC (e.g.,
loudspeaker) or BC (e.g., vibrator) before CC was found. For
example, most people may believe that an earphone produces
only airborne sound, even if the vibration of the earphone body
may be transmitted to aural cartilages or temporal bones.
Hence, an earphone is generally identified to be AC equipment.
Auditory devices are usually classified according to the method
of sound conduction with the highest percentage among the
three types of sound conduction. Therefore, it is correct to
classify earphones as AC devices because AC is the dominant
method of conduction in it.
To avoid confusion, we should mention another factor about
the naming of acoustic devices. For example, when a patient
with aural atresia hears sounds from a loudspeaker, the pathway
of sound conduction could be BC or CC, because he or she
cannot hear the sound through AC. However, almost all the
sound information is conveyed by AC, therefore a loud speaker
is identified as an AC sound device. If we refer to a loudspeaker
as not AC, but BC or CC, when a patient with aural atresia uses
it, it is confusing because the same loudspeaker is sometimes
called an AC device and sometimes called a BC or CC device.
In view of this, we should not use different names for the same
device even if the naming is wrong according to the mechanism
in a certain situation.
The classification of conductions in Table 1 applies to
individuals with a normal external auditory canal. The
discrepancy between the naming of sound conduction and
the classification in Table 1 does not occur in connection with
such individuals. However, we also have to consider individuals
with an abnormal external auditory canal, and we must decide
on the naming rules to prevent calling the same device by
different names depending on the situation. The two abnormal
cases are described below.
Morimoto et al. [17] reported that patients with fibrotic
atresia of the external auditory canal could hear clear sound
using CC devices, and so used the term “fibrotic tissue
pathway”. In that case, the filled soft tissue in the external
auditory canal mediates the vibrational connection to the
ossicles in the middle ear, so the sound transmission pathway is
different from the cartilage-air pathway in normal ears.
However, the fibrotic tissue pathway is also categorized within
the CC concept in Table 1, because a fibrotic tissue pathway
fulfills the definition of CC in normal ears, i.e. a vibrator
contacts aural cartilage, and vibration of the skull bone is
unnecessary. It also means this pathway is different from
cartilage-bone pathway.
Nishimura et al. [24] made it clear that a CC hearing aid
is also useful for patients with aural bony atresia. In that
case, it is impossible to transmit the sound to the cochlea
through the cartilage-air pathway, so cranial-bone vibration
is needed. According to the classification in Table 1, this
case is neither CC nor BC, because a vibrator contacts
aural cartilage and cranial bone is vibrated. However, we
consider this case to be CC, because this is one of the CC
pathways, i.e. the cartilage-bone pathway. This definition
can avoid inconveniences in daily life, because people do
not need to change the name of a device, for example a
hearing aid, according to the user’s normal or abnormal
external auditory canal.
Regarding the efficiency of sound conduction, cartilage-
bone pathway is more efficient than bone conduction and less
efficient than fibrotic tissue pathway. We speculate that the
cartilage plays a role of an adaptor for good efficiency of sound
conduction like an implant in BAHA. Fixation between the
cartilage and the bone is strong and adhesion area is large in
addition to relatively thin bony tissue may make good sound
conduction efficiency. In the case of fibrotic tissue pathway, the
energy of transducer does not need to use for vibrating temporal
bone, therefore it can realize better efficiency than cartilage-
bone pathway.
9. Conclusion
Our 
series 
of 
studies 
has 
identified 
some 
different
characteristics between CC and the well-known AC and BC.
As CC does not belong within the concepts of the other two
forms of conduction, we would like to propose a novel concept
for CC, as the third sound-transmission pathway. Using CC in
hearing aids would benefit patients with disorders in the outer
ear, and CC could help realize new smartphones, with
advantages including easy volume control and quick improve-
ment of S/N, as well as various other types of new
communication devices. Taking the new CC concept into
consideration, we propose a new classification scheme for
sound conduction pathways from the sound origin to the inner
ear for subjects with a normal external auditory canal.
