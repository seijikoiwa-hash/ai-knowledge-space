A Generalization of Brouwer's Fixed Point Theorem

By Shizuo Kakutani
Received January 21, 1941  |  Institute for Advanced Study

The purpose of the present paper is to give a generalization of Brouwer's fixed point theorem, and to show that this generalized theorem implies the theorems of J. von Neumann obtained by him in connection with the theory of games and mathematical economics.

1. Generalized Fixed Point Theorem

The fixed point theorem of Brouwer reads as follows: if \(x \mapsto \varphi(x)\) is a continuous point-to-point mapping of an \(r\)-dimensional closed simplex \(S\) into itself, then there exists an \(x_0 \in S\) such that \(x_0=\varphi(x_0)\).

This theorem can be generalized in the following way. Let \(\mathcal{S}(S)\) be the family of all closed convex subsets of \(S\). A point-to-set mapping \(x \mapsto \Phi(x)\) of \(S\) into \(\mathcal{S}(S)\) is called upper semi-continuous if \(x_n \to x_0\), \(y_n \in \Phi(x_n)\), and \(y_n \to y_0\) imply \(y_0 \in \Phi(x_0)\). This condition is equivalent to saying that the graph of \(\Phi(x)\), namely \(\sum_x x\times \Phi(x)\), is a closed subset of \(S\times S\).

Theorem 1. If \(x\mapsto \Phi(x)\) is an upper semi-continuous point-to-set mapping of an \(r\)-dimensional closed simplex \(S\) into \(\mathcal{S}(S)\), then there exists an \(x_0\in S\) such that \(x_0\in \Phi(x_0)\).

Proof. Let \(S^{(n)}\) be the \(n\)-th barycentric simplicial subdivision of \(S\). For each vertex \(x_n\) of \(S^{(n)}\), take an arbitrary point \(y_n\) from \(\Phi(x_n)\). The mapping \(x_n\mapsto y_n\), defined on all vertices of \(S^{(n)}\), extends linearly inside each simplex of \(S^{(n)}\) and gives a continuous point-to-point mapping \(x\mapsto \varphi_n(x)\) of \(S\) into itself. By Brouwer's fixed point theorem, there exists \(x_n\in S\) such that \(x_n=\varphi_n(x_n)\).

Taking a subsequence of \(\{x_n\}\) converging to a point \(x_0\in S\), Kakutani shows that this \(x_0\) is the required point. Let \(\Delta_n\) be an \(r\)-dimensional simplex of \(S^{(n)}\) containing \(x_n\), with vertices \(x_n^0,x_n^1,\ldots,x_n^r\). Then \(x_n^i\to x_0\) for each \(i\), and

\[x_n=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\lambda_n^i x_n^i,\qquad \lambda_n^i\ge 0, \qquad \sum_{i=0}^{r}\lambda_n^i=1.\]

Putting \(y_n^i=\varphi_n(x_n^i)\), one has \(y_n^i\in\Phi(x_n^i)\) and

\[x_n=\varphi_n(x_n)=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\lambda_n^i y_n^i.\]

After passing to a further subsequence, assume that \(y_n^i\to y^i\) and \(\lambda_n^i\to\lambda^i\). Then \(\lambda^i\ge0\), \(\sum_i\lambda^i=1\), and \(x_0=\sum_i\lambda^i y^i\). Since \(x_n^i\to x_0\), \(y_n^i\in\Phi(x_n^i)\), and \(y_n^i\to y^i\), upper semi-continuity gives \(y^i\in\Phi(x_0)\). By convexity of \(\Phi(x_0)\), \(x_0\in\Phi(x_0)\). This completes the proof.

Remark. Brouwer's fixed point theorem is a special case of Theorem 1 when each \(\Phi(x)\) consists of only one point \(\varphi(x)\). In that case, upper semi-continuity of \(\Phi\) is simply continuity of \(\varphi\).

Corollary. Theorem 1 is also valid when \(S\) is an arbitrary bounded closed convex set in a Euclidean space.

Proof. Take a closed simplex \(S'\) containing \(S\), and consider a continuous retracting point-to-point mapping \(x\mapsto \psi(x)\) of \(S'\) onto \(S\). Then \(x\mapsto \Phi(\psi(x))\) is an upper semi-continuous point-to-set mapping of \(S'\) into \(\mathcal{S}(S')\). Applying Theorem 1 gives the desired fixed point in \(S\).

2. Common Point Theorem

Theorem 2. Let \(K\) and \(L\) be two bounded closed convex sets in Euclidean spaces \(R^m\) and \(R^n\), respectively, and consider their Cartesian product \(K\times L\). Let \(U\) and \(V\) be two closed subsets of \(K\times L\) such that for any \(x_0\in K\), the section \(U_{x_0}\) of all \(y\in L\) with \((x_0,y)\in U\) is non-empty, closed, and convex; and for any \(y_0\in L\), the section \(V_{y_0}\) of all \(x\in K\) with \((x,y_0)\in V\) is non-empty, closed, and convex. Under these assumptions, \(U\) and \(V\) have a common point.

Proof. Put \(S=K\times L\), and define a point-to-set mapping \(z\mapsto\Phi(z)\) by \(\Phi(z)=V_y\times U_x\) if \(z=(x,y)\). Since \(U\) and \(V\) are closed, \(\Phi(z)\) is upper semi-continuous. By the corollary to Theorem 1, there exists \(z_0=(x_0,y_0)\in K\times L\) such that \(z_0\in\Phi(z_0)\). Hence \(x_0\in V_{y_0}\) and \(y_0\in U_{x_0}\), which means \((x_0,y_0)\in U\cap V\). Thus the proof is completed.

Remark. Theorem 2 is due to J. von Neumann, who proved it by using a notion of integral in Euclidean spaces. Kakutani notes that the proof above is simpler. This theorem has applications to mathematical economics.

3. Minimax Theorem

Theorem 3. Let \(f(x,y)\) be a continuous real-valued function defined for \(x\in K\) and \(y\in L\), where \(K\) and \(L\) are arbitrary bounded closed convex sets in two Euclidean spaces. If for every \(x_0\in K\) and every real number \(\alpha\), the set of all \(y\in L\) such that \(f(x_0,y)\le \alpha\) is convex, and if for every \(y_0\in L\) and every real number \(\beta\), the set of all \(x\in K\) such that \(f(x,y_0)\ge \beta\) is convex, then

\[\max_{x\in K}\min_{y\in L} f(x,y)=\min_{y\in L}\max_{x\in K} f(x,y).\]

Proof. Let \(U\) be the set of all \((x_0,y_0)\in K\times L\) such that \(f(x_0,y_0)=\min_{y\in L}f(x_0,y)\), and let \(V\) be the set of all \((x_0,y_0)\in K\times L\) such that \(f(x_0,y_0)=\max_{x\in K}f(x,y_0)\). These sets satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem 2. Therefore, there is a point \((x_0,y_0)\in U\cap V\), so

\[f(x_0,y_0)=\min_{y\in L} f(x_0,y)=\max_{x\in K} f(x,y_0).\]

It follows that

\[\min_{y\in L}\max_{x\in K} f(x,y) \le \max_{x\in K}f(x,y_0)=f(x_0,y_0)=\min_{y\in L}f(x_0,y) \le \max_{x\in K}\min_{y\in L}f(x,y).\]

Since the reverse inequality \(\min_y\max_x f(x,y)\ge\max_x\min_y f(x,y)\) is clear, equality follows.

Remark. Theorem 3 is one of the fundamental theorems in the theory of games developed by J. von Neumann.

In concluding this paper, Kakutani expresses his hearty thanks to Dr. A. D. Wallace for kind discussions on this problem. Wallace had also obtained analogous results for trees.
