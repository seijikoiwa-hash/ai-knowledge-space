The History of Drug Development: 50 Years Ago, Today, and the Future

1. Introduction (Including a Decade of Experience)

Drug development has shifted its center of gravity from the “hunt for substances” to the “design of information.” Fifty years ago, the process was largely linear—“synthesis → animal → human.” Today, we are entering an era in which we do not simply make molecules; we deliver blueprints. What, fundamentally, changed over those decades is that hypothesis and accountability became prerequisites. “It worked, so it’s fine” is no longer acceptable; at the entry point we are asked to explain why it should work and why adverse effects might occur. The maturation of targeted therapies, the evolution of antibodies, and the practical arrival of nucleic-acid medicines have quietly—but decisively—rewritten the drug-discovery paradigm[note22].

Figure 1: in vitro[note1] / in vivo[note2] / in silico[note3] ── Fusion of research arenas and methodologies

That shift is embodied by the rise of in silico. Computational science has joined experimental science (in vitro/in vivo) as a third pillar. More importantly, it is no longer a mere “support” function; it is becoming a primary driver that decides which experiments not to run—before any wet work begins. This essay traces the groping trial-and-error of 50 years ago, today’s data-driven science, and the future: a development process in which AI designs and humans remain responsible.

2. How Drugs Are Made (50 Years Ago, Today, Future)

■ 50 Years Ago: Accumulation of Chance and Experience

In the 1970s, development was led by small molecules. Teams screened libraries of natural products and synthetic compounds, then advanced “whatever worked.” Acute toxicity[note4] and subacute toxicity[note5] were assessed in animals; if nothing obvious emerged, the compound moved into humans. The most hazardous step was first-in-human dosing (what we now call Phase I[note9]): nonclinical packages were coarse, dose selection was largely empirical, and in practice it often came down to “you don’t know until you try it in people.” Failures were rarely shared, and different companies repeatedly made the same mistakes.

Figure 2 (Today): Hypothesis-Driven & Parallel Optimization Model — efficacy, safety, and pharmacokinetics are optimized in parallel before the IND gate

■ Today: The Era of Hypothesis-Driven & Parallel Optimization

Today’s (2020s) process retains the same broad skeleton—“discovery → nonclinical → clinical[note6]”—but the content has changed completely. The key difference is that strategy comes first. Unless you can articulate a biological hypothesis—why it should work, and for whom—you cannot even enter development. In nonclinical work, efficacy, safety, and pharmacokinetics[note7] are optimized in parallel. The IND gate (Investigational New Drug Application; Note[note8]) is institutionalized, and you cannot proceed unless you can rationally justify a safe first-in-human starting dose. Phase I[note9] focuses on safety; Phase II[note10] seeks proof of concept[note11]. In practice, the fate of a program is often decided in Phase II.

“Modern development workflows are built not only to be scientifically sound, but to be explainable when they fail.”

■ Future: The Era of Design ── Designing Cells, AI Exploring

So what comes next? The answer is not simply “a new molecule.” In many cases, what follows RNA therapeutics[note23] will be medicines that design cells: gene editing[note20] and cell therapies[note21]. The target space expands to DNA and cellular function itself. CRISPR[note12]-based editing and CAR-T[note13]-type therapies embody the idea of delivering “living factories.” At the leading edge of that design sits AI—not as the drug, but as the designer that searches for optimal sequences, optimal CAR architectures[note24], and optimal delivery vectors[note14].

3. Where the Differences Lie

■ Fundamental Rupture in Development Philosophy

The difference between 50 years ago, today, and the future can be summarized as “experience and intuition” versus “hypothesis and accountability.” Fifty years ago, “you won’t know until you try” was the default. Today, “you must not proceed without understanding” is embedded in regulation and practice. The future shifts further: AI will take on regions humans cannot realistically design, while humans concentrate on responsibility and ethical judgment.

■ The Essence of Modality Evolution: Division of Labor, Not Replacement

The “small molecules → antibodies → RNA” storyline is convenient, but it is not a history of replacement. It is a history of specialization: each modality has come to own a different target space. Small molecules address intracellular pockets; antibodies address extracellular targets and membrane surfaces; RNA targets intracellular mRNA. In other words, the toolbox expanded rather than swapped out. (Modality[note16])

■ Failures of 50 Years Ago and Failures Today

The most common failure 50 years ago was “it doesn’t work in humans—or it’s unsafe.” Animal models were crude, and target concepts were often vague. Today, the most frequent failure is still lack of efficacy, but the failure modes are more scientific: an incorrect disease hypothesis, poor patient selection, or incorrect dose selection. Attrition due to overt toxicity has decreased; in exchange, the precision of the underlying hypothesis is scrutinized far more harshly.

4. AI Involvement ── Changing the Very Order of Development

■ AI Belongs to in silico and Intervenes in in vitro / in vivo

AI is not merely a step forward in computation; it changes the “arena” of research. AI’s home is in silico, yet its influence reaches deeply into in vitro and in vivo. In vitro, it drives image analysis and laboratory automation; in vivo, it supports imaging, clinical decision support, and real-world data analysis. AI acts as an “information adhesive” that binds the three arenas together. The key point is this: AI should not make the final judgment; it should handle the selection and design that precede judgment. In that sense, intervening[note27] in in vitro/in vivo means deliberately acting on the stages and locations where biological phenomena unfold.

“As a methodology rooted in in silico, AI can be deeply involved in the design, analysis, and triage of in vitro and in vivo work—but it must not become the agent of judgment or responsibility.”

■ The Order AI Changes ── From "Experiment, Then Think" to "Think, Then Experiment"

Fifty years ago, progress was driven by trial and error: “experiment → observe → next experiment.” Today, AI can generate hypotheses before experiments are run. Generative models can propose novel structures, predict toxicity, and run digital twins[note17] of clinical trials—dramatically reducing wasted experiments. Still, human in vivo confirmation can never be eliminated. What can be reduced is “unnecessary” in vivo work; the importance of in vivo itself, if anything, increases.

■ The Line AI Must Not Cross

This is the most important point. AI must never cross the following lines:

  • Final judgment on patient safety ── Decisions to continue administration must not be made based on AI recommendations.
  • Interpretation of biological causality ── Correlations found by AI might be spurious correlations. Meaning-making must be done by humans.
  • Data integrity and auditability for GxP[note18] ── Traceability must not be broken by AI's automatic generation.
  • Ethical judgment and accountability ── "Because AI said so" will not be accepted by society.
Figure 5: AI Drug Discovery ── Transition from experimental science to data-driven science

AI has changed the order of development at its root. Linear stages no longer capture reality. Drug discovery is becoming a spiral process: design in silico, narrow in vitro, confirm in vivo. That cycle is accelerating year by year. In the future, researchers will reclaim time to think, while AI shoulders the work of searching.

5. Conclusion

Fifty years ago, drug development was a craft built on experience. It depended on chance discoveries, and hazards often became visible only in humans. Today, development has become a science of hypothesis and accountability. Rigorous gates sit between nonclinical and clinical stages, and failure is increasingly captured as institutional knowledge. The future will move into a phase in which AI designs and humans remain responsible—an era of redesigning cells and rewriting genes. What will ultimately be tested is our philosophy: how far we should intervene, and where we must stop.

“A drug is not an all-purpose tool. It is knowledge for deciding when to use it—and when to refrain. Side effects will not go to zero, aging will not be defeated, and there will be times when the right choice is not to medicate—because human beings are not entities that can be fully controlled.”

AI can help us navigate that imperfection, but it must never become the subject of judgment. Japan leads in basic research and in selected modalities such as ADCs[note19], yet it lags in industrialization and the circulation of global capital. In the AI era, Japan’s path to competitiveness depends on leveraging strengths in high-quality data, precision, and process thinking—and making strategic investments to secure platform dominance[note28].
