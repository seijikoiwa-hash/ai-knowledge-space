    Abstract of Saussure’s Core Theory
    — A Structural Summary of Course in General Linguistics —

    Ferdinand de Saussure fundamentally transformed the study of language by redefining language not as a collection of names attached to things, but as a social system of differences and relations. His work became the foundation not only of modern linguistics, but also of structuralism, semiotics, anthropology, psychoanalysis, media theory, and later post-structuralism.

    Saussure begins by criticizing earlier forms of language study. Traditional grammar, inherited from the Greeks and developed mainly by the French, was normative rather than scientific. Its purpose was merely to distinguish correct forms from incorrect ones, not to investigate language itself. Philology, while more historical and textual, remained excessively attached to written language and neglected living speech. Comparative philology opened a new field by comparing Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, and related languages, but even it failed to define the true object of linguistics.

    The Problem of Defining the Object of Linguistics

    For Saussure, no science can develop a method without first defining its object. Yet language is uniquely difficult because it appears differently depending on the viewpoint adopted. A word may be considered:

    • as sound,
    • as concept,
    • as historical form,
    • as grammatical structure,
    • or as psychological phenomenon.

    Thus the object does not precede the viewpoint; rather, the viewpoint creates the object.

    Saussure therefore argues that the total phenomenon of human speech is too heterogeneous to serve directly as the object of science. Speech contains physical, physiological, psychological, individual, and social dimensions simultaneously.

    Langage, Langue, and Parole

    Consequently, he isolates a specific domain within the totality of speech:

    French TermMeaning
    langagehuman language activity as a whole
    languethe social language system
    paroleindividual acts of speaking

    Langue becomes the proper object of linguistics because it alone possesses internal unity and systematic structure.

    The Speech Circuit

    To explain this, Saussure introduces the famous “speech circuit.”

    When one person speaks:

    1. a concept arises in the brain,
    2. the concept becomes associated with a sound-image,
    3. the vocal organs produce sound,
    4. sound waves travel physically,
    5. the listener hears them,
    6. and the listener reconstructs the corresponding concept.

    The crucial point is that language does not consist of physical sounds themselves, but of “sound-images.”

    A sound-image is not a material sound but the psychological imprint of sound. Language is therefore fundamentally psychological and social rather than merely physical.

    Saussure further insists that language never exists fully within a single individual. It exists only collectively. Each speaker possesses merely a partial internalization of a larger social system distributed throughout the community. Language is thus a collective structure deposited in the minds of speakers.

    The Linguistic Sign

    The fundamental unit of this system is the sign.

    Saussure rejects the ordinary assumption that a linguistic sign links a thing and a name. Instead, the sign unites:

    ComponentFunction
    signifierthe sound-image
    signifiedthe concept

    The sign therefore does not connect language directly to reality. Rather, it joins a psychological sound-pattern to a conceptual content.

    The Arbitrariness of the Sign

    From this follows Saussure’s principle of arbitrariness.

    There is no natural connection between a signifier and its signified. The sounds:

    • dog,
    • chien,
    • Hund,
    • inu,

    have nothing inherently “dog-like” about them. Their relation to the concept is socially established, not naturally determined.

    However, arbitrariness does not mean personal freedom. Individual speakers cannot alter signs at will. The linguistic system precedes and constrains them.

    Linguistic Value and Difference

    Saussure’s theory becomes even more radical when he discusses value.

    Ordinarily one assumes that words possess meaning internally. Saussure denies this. A word acquires value only through its relations and oppositions to other words.

    A linguistic term is defined not by positive content but by difference.

    For example, the meaning of “sheep” depends on its distinction from:

    • goat,
    • cow,
    • wolf,
    • lamb,
    • and other neighboring terms.

    Meaning therefore emerges relationally rather than essentially.

    Saussure compares language to chess.

    The value of a chess piece does not derive from the wood or ivory composing it. Its value depends entirely upon its position and relations within the system of the game. Likewise, linguistic units possess value only through structural relations.

    This leads to Saussure’s most famous and radical claim:

    “In language there are only differences without positive terms.”

    Language contains neither pre-existing ideas nor pre-existing sounds. What exists are only conceptual and phonic differences generated within the system itself.

    Thus meaning is not substance. Meaning is produced by:

    • relations,
    • oppositions,
    • differences,
    • and positions within a structure.

    Language becomes a differential system rather than a naming system.

    Semiology

    Saussure extends this insight into a general science of signs, which he calls semiology.

    Language is only one sign-system among others:

    • writing,
    • rituals,
    • military signals,
    • symbolic ceremonies,
    • social codes.

    Linguistics therefore becomes part of a broader science studying signs within society.

    Internal and External Linguistics

    Saussure also insists on distinguishing internal from external linguistics.

    Political history, geography, colonization, literature, and institutions certainly affect language historically, but they remain external unless they alter the internal structure of the system itself.

    Again he uses the analogy of chess:

    • changing the material of the chess pieces is external,
    • changing the number or function of pieces is internal.

    Internal linguistics studies transformations of the system itself.

    The Consequence of Saussure’s Theory

    The consequence of Saussure’s theory was immense.

    Before Saussure, language was generally understood as a naming system attached to reality. After Saussure, language became understood as a structure of relations.

    This transformation made possible:

    • structural anthropology (Lévi-Strauss),
    • psychoanalytic structuralism (Lacan),
    • semiotics (Barthes),
    • deconstruction (Derrida),
    • and much of twentieth-century theory.

    Remarkably, Saussure’s model also anticipates aspects of modern AI language systems.

    Large language models do not store meanings as fixed substances attached to words. Instead, meanings emerge statistically from relational positions within vast networks of differences and contextual associations.

    In this sense, Saussure’s insight that linguistic value arises through differential relations remains strikingly contemporary.

    Saussure was therefore not merely a linguist.

    He redefined the nature of meaning itself.
