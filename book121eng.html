On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies

English Translation of Einstein’s 1905 Paper

Original title: Zur Elektrodynamik bewegter Körper
Author: Albert Einstein
Published: Annalen der Physik, 1905

Page 1 Page 2 Page 3 Page 4 Page 5
Page 1

§1 Introduction

It is well known that Maxwell’s electrodynamics — as usually understood at the present time — when applied to moving bodies leads to asymmetries which do not appear to be inherent in the phenomena themselves.

Consider, for example, the reciprocal electrodynamic action of a magnet and a conductor. The observable phenomenon depends here only on the relative motion of conductor and magnet, while the customary view sharply distinguishes the two cases according to which of the two bodies is regarded as moving.

If the magnet moves while the conductor remains at rest, there arises in the neighborhood of the magnet an electric field possessing a definite energy, producing a current in places where parts of the conductor are located.

But if the magnet is stationary and the conductor moves, no electric field arises around the magnet. In the conductor, however, we find an electromotive force corresponding in magnitude to the electric forces present in the first case. This force gives rise to the same current as in the former case.

Examples of this sort, together with unsuccessful attempts to discover any motion of the Earth relative to the “light medium,” suggest that the phenomena of electrodynamics as well as mechanics possess no properties corresponding to the idea of absolute rest.

They suggest rather that the same laws of electrodynamics and optics will hold for all frames of reference for which the equations of mechanics are valid.

We shall elevate this conjecture — hereafter called the “Principle of Relativity” — to the status of a postulate, and introduce in addition another postulate: that light is always propagated in empty space with a definite velocity V independent of the state of motion of the emitting body.

These two postulates suffice for the attainment of a simple and consistent theory of the electrodynamics of moving bodies, based on Maxwell’s theory for stationary bodies.

I. Kinematical Part

§1 Definition of Simultaneity

Let us take a coordinate system in which Newton’s equations hold good. To render our presentation more precise and to distinguish this system verbally from others, we call it the “stationary system.”

If a material point is at rest relative to this coordinate system, its position may be defined by rigid measuring rods and Euclidean geometry, and may be expressed in Cartesian coordinates.

If we wish to describe the motion of a material point, we give the values of its coordinates as functions of time.

However, it must be noted that all judgments involving time are always judgments concerning simultaneous events.

For example, when we say: “The train arrives here at 7 o’clock,” this means: “The pointing of the small hand of my watch to 7 and the arrival of the train are simultaneous events.”

At first sight it might appear possible to overcome all difficulties concerning the definition of time by replacing “time” with “the position of the small hand of my watch.” Indeed, such a definition suffices when time has to be defined only for the place where the watch is located.

But the definition is no longer satisfactory when a series of events occurring at different places must be temporally connected.

Page 3

Synchronization of Clocks

Suppose that at point A of space there is a clock, and likewise at point B another clock identical in every respect with the clock at A.

If a light signal is sent from A to B at time tA, reflected at B at time tB, and returns to A at time tA′, then the clocks are synchronous when:

tB − tA = tA′ − tB

We assume that this definition of synchronization can be carried out consistently for arbitrary points in the stationary system.

Accordingly, we define:

  • If the clock at B synchronizes with the clock at A, then the clock at A synchronizes with the clock at B.
  • If the clock at A is synchronous with the clock at B, and the clock at B with the clock at C, then A and C are also synchronous.

Thus we arrive at a definition of simultaneity for events occurring at different places.

We further assume that:

2AB / (tA′ − tA) = V

where V is a universal constant — the velocity of light in empty space.

§2 On the Relativity of Lengths and Times

The following considerations are based upon the Principle of Relativity and the Principle of the Constancy of the Velocity of Light.

We formulate these principles as follows:

  1. The laws according to which the states of physical systems change are independent of which of two coordinate systems moving uniformly relative to one another is chosen.
  2. Every ray of light moves in the stationary system with the fixed velocity V, independent of whether this ray of light is emitted by a moving or a stationary body.

Hence:

velocity = light path / time interval

Consider a rigid rod at rest. Let its length measured in the stationary system be l.

We imagine this rod placed along the X-axis of the stationary system and seek its length when it moves with velocity v along the axis.

Two different procedures are possible:

  • The observer moves together with the measuring rod and measures directly with a measuring rod at rest relative to him.
  • The observer determines simultaneously the positions of the endpoints of the moving rod in the stationary system.

The two methods do not in general yield the same result.

Page 5

Moving Clocks and Time Dilation

Imagine furthermore that at the two ends A and B of the moving rod there are clocks synchronized in the stationary system.

Suppose a light signal travels from A to B and back again. Taking into account the constancy of the velocity of light, we find:

tB − tA = rAB / (V − v)
tA′ − tB = rAB / (V + v)

Hence the clocks synchronized in the stationary system do not appear synchronous to an observer moving with the rod.

We therefore conclude:

Two events that are simultaneous in one frame of reference are not necessarily simultaneous in another frame moving relative to the first.

This is the fundamental idea of the relativity of simultaneity.

The notions of absolute time and absolute simultaneity lose their universal meaning.

§3 Theory of the Transformation of Coordinates and Times

Let a stationary coordinate system K be given, together with another system k moving uniformly along the X-axis with velocity v relative to K.

We seek the equations connecting the coordinates and times of an event measured in the two systems.

At time t = 0 the origins of both systems coincide. A light signal emitted from the common origin propagates according to:

x² + y² + z² = V²t²

In the moving system the same propagation law must hold:

ξ² + η² + ζ² = V²τ²

Since the equations are linear, we seek transformations of the form:

τ = φ(v)β(t − vx/V²)
ξ = φ(v)β(x − vt)
η = φ(v)y
ζ = φ(v)z

where:

β = 1 / √(1 − (v²/V²))

and φ(v) is at first an unknown function of velocity.

Page 7

Determination of the Transformation Equations

To determine φ(v), we apply the transformation successively from K to k and then back again from k to K.

Since the two transformations must reproduce the identity transformation, we obtain:

φ(v)φ(−v) = 1

By symmetry considerations:

φ(v) = φ(−v)

Hence:

φ(v) = 1

The transformation equations therefore become:

τ = β(t − vx/V²)
ξ = β(x − vt)
η = y
ζ = z

These are the transformation equations connecting the coordinates and times of two inertial systems in relative motion.

Today these equations are known as the Lorentz Transformation.

Page 8

§4 Physical Meaning of the Transformation Equations

Consider a sphere at rest in the stationary system K with equation:

x² + y² + z² = R²

Viewed from the moving system, the equation becomes:

x² / (1 − v²/V²) + y² + z² = R²

Thus the moving sphere appears contracted in the direction of motion.

The dimension parallel to the motion is shortened by the factor:

√(1 − v²/V²)

while the dimensions perpendicular to the motion remain unchanged.

This phenomenon is known as the Lorentz contraction.

The theory further predicts that moving clocks run more slowly than stationary clocks.

If a clock moves with velocity v relative to the observer, then the elapsed time measured by that clock is reduced by the factor:

√(1 − v²/V²)

Thus:

A moving clock runs more slowly than a stationary clock.
Page 9

Relativity of Simultaneity

Suppose two events occur simultaneously at different locations in the stationary system.

Applying the Lorentz transformation to these events shows that in the moving system the times are no longer equal.

Hence simultaneity depends upon the state of motion of the observer.

There is therefore no universal or absolute time valid for all observers.

Each inertial system possesses its own time coordinate.

This conclusion fundamentally changes the classical concepts of space and time.

Space and time are no longer independent entities. Instead they are interconnected through the transformation equations.

Page 10

§5 Addition Theorem of Velocities

Let a point move in the moving system k with velocity components:

wξ , wη

Transforming to the stationary system K gives:

U = (v + w) / (1 + vw/V²)

for motion along the X-axis.

This equation replaces the classical rule of addition of velocities.

If both velocities are smaller than V, then the resulting velocity is also smaller than V.

In particular, if:

w = V

then:

U = V

Thus the velocity of light remains invariant under the addition of velocities.

No material body can exceed the velocity of light.

II. Electrodynamical Part

§6 Transformation of Maxwell–Hertz Equations

We now pass from kinematics to electrodynamics.

The Maxwell–Hertz equations for empty space are assumed valid in the stationary system K.

Let:

(X, Y, Z)

represent the electric force components, and:

(L, M, N)

represent the magnetic force components.

Applying the Lorentz transformation to the electromagnetic equations yields the transformed field equations for the moving system k.

The transformed electric field components become:

X′ = X
Y′ = β(Y − vN/V)
Z′ = β(Z + vM/V)

and the magnetic field transforms as:

L′ = L
M′ = β(M + vZ/V)
N′ = β(N − vY/V)

These equations demonstrate that electric and magnetic fields are not independent entities.

A purely electric field in one frame may appear partly magnetic in another frame.

Electricity and magnetism are therefore unified aspects of a single electromagnetic reality.

Page 12

Interpretation of the Electromagnetic Transformations

Consider an electric charge at rest in the stationary system.

In the stationary system, only an electric field exists around the charge.

However, for an observer moving relative to the charge, both electric and magnetic fields are present.

The magnetic field arises naturally from the transformation laws.

Thus magnetism is not an independent phenomenon separate from electricity.

Instead, magnetic effects are consequences of the relativistic transformation of electric fields.

This resolves the asymmetry discussed at the beginning of the paper concerning magnets and conductors.

The observable phenomena depend only upon relative motion.

No absolute state of rest is required.

§7 Theory of the Doppler Principle and Aberration

Suppose that an observer moves relative to a distant light source.

The observed frequency of light differs from the emitted frequency because of the relative motion between source and observer.

Einstein derives the relativistic Doppler formula:

ν′ = ν (1 − (v/V) cos φ) / √(1 − v²/V²)

where:

  • ν is the emitted frequency
  • ν′ is the observed frequency
  • φ is the angle between the observer’s motion and the incoming light ray

For motion directly toward the source:

ν′ = ν √((1 − v/V)/(1 + v/V))

Einstein also derives the aberration formula:

cos φ′ = (cos φ − v/V) / (1 − (v/V) cos φ)

This describes how the apparent direction of incoming light changes because of the observer’s motion.

Page 14

Transformation of Light Energy

The amplitude and intensity of light also transform according to the Lorentz equations.

If A represents the amplitude measured in the stationary system, then in the moving system:

A′² = A² (1 − (v/V) cos φ)² / (1 − v²/V²)

Hence the observed intensity depends upon the relative motion between source and observer.

A light source approached by the observer appears brighter.

A receding source appears dimmer.

Einstein remarks that the energy and frequency of a light complex vary according to the same transformation law.

This observation later became important for the development of quantum theory.

Page 15

§8 Transformation of Radiation Pressure

Einstein next investigates the pressure exerted by light upon reflecting surfaces.

The incident and reflected waves are transformed between coordinate systems using the same relativistic equations.

The radiation pressure P on a reflecting surface becomes:

P = 2(A² / 8π) ((cos φ − v/V)² / (1 − v²/V²))

In the approximation of low velocities this reduces to the classical expression.

Einstein emphasizes that optical phenomena involving moving bodies can be analyzed entirely through the Lorentz transformation.

No ether is required.

The laws of electrodynamics remain valid in every inertial frame.

Page 16

§9 Transformation of Maxwell–Hertz Equations with Convection Currents

Einstein now extends the previous analysis to include electric charges in motion.

The equations now contain convection currents produced by moving electricity.

Let:

ρ

represent the electric charge density, and:

(u_x, u_y, u_z)

represent the velocity components of the moving charges.

Applying the Lorentz transformation to these quantities yields transformed equations valid in the moving system.

The transformed charge density becomes:

ρ′ = β (1 − vu_x/V²) ρ

while the transformed velocity components follow from the relativistic addition law of velocities.

Einstein concludes that the electrodynamics of moving bodies follows consistently from the Principle of Relativity together with Maxwell’s equations.

The theory requires no preferred frame and no stationary ether.

§10 Dynamics of the Slowly Accelerated Electron

Einstein now investigates the motion of an electron in an electromagnetic field.

He considers an electron moving slowly relative to the speed of light.

Let:

(X, Y, Z)

represent the electric force components acting on the electron.

The equations of motion are:

μ d²x/dt² = εX
μ d²y/dt² = εY
μ d²z/dt² = εZ

where:

  • μ is the mass of the electron
  • ε is the electric charge

Einstein transforms these equations into a moving coordinate system and investigates how inertia depends upon velocity.

Page 18

Longitudinal and Transverse Mass

The transformed equations reveal that the effective inertia of the electron depends upon the direction of acceleration relative to the motion.

Einstein defines:

  • Longitudinal mass — for acceleration parallel to motion
  • Transverse mass — for acceleration perpendicular to motion

The longitudinal mass becomes:

m_l = μ / (1 − v²/V²)^(3/2)

The transverse mass becomes:

m_t = μ / √(1 − v²/V²)

Thus inertia increases with velocity.

As the velocity approaches the speed of light, the mass tends toward infinity.

Therefore no material body can reach or exceed the velocity of light.

Page 19

Kinetic Energy of the Electron

Einstein next derives the kinetic energy of the moving electron.

Integrating the work performed by the electric force yields:

W = μV² { 1 / √(1 − v²/V²) − 1 }

This expression differs from the classical Newtonian kinetic energy formula.

For small velocities it reduces approximately to:

W ≈ (1/2) μv²

However, at high velocities relativistic corrections become essential.

The kinetic energy grows without bound as the velocity approaches V.

Again, this confirms the impossibility of surpassing the speed of light.

Page 20

Experimental Consequences

Einstein discusses several experimental consequences of the theory.

The deflection of electrons by electric and magnetic fields depends upon the relativistic increase of inertia.

Measurements of cathode rays and rapidly moving charged particles therefore provide tests of the theory.

The ratio between electric and magnetic deflection changes according to:

A_m / A_e = v / V

The theory predicts precise quantitative deviations from classical electrodynamics.

Such effects become significant only for velocities approaching the speed of light.

Einstein further derives the relation between kinetic energy and the potential difference traversed by the electron.

These results establish a new relativistic mechanics consistent with electrodynamics.

Page 21

Final Remarks

Einstein concludes by emphasizing that the electrodynamics of moving bodies can be understood entirely through:

  • The Principle of Relativity
  • The Constancy of the Velocity of Light

No luminiferous ether is necessary.

The concepts of absolute space and absolute time lose their physical meaning.

The laws of physics possess the same form in all inertial frames.

Space and time become interconnected quantities linked by the Lorentz transformation.

The theory provides a unified description of mechanics, optics, and electrodynamics.

Einstein closes the paper with an acknowledgment:

“In conclusion, I note that while working on the problem treated here, my friend and colleague M. Besso stood faithfully by my side, and I am indebted to him for several valuable suggestions.”

Bern, June 1905.

Supplementary Notes on the Meaning of Relativity

The consequences of the theory extend beyond electrodynamics alone.

The relativity principle changes the conceptual foundations of physics itself.

Before Einstein, space and time were regarded as absolute entities existing independently of observation.

In the new theory, however, measurements of space and time depend upon the motion of the observer.

Lengths contract. Clocks slow down. Events judged simultaneous in one frame are not simultaneous in another.

These effects are not optical illusions or measurement errors. They are genuine structural properties of spacetime.

The Lorentz transformation therefore replaces the Galilean transformation as the fundamental symmetry of mechanics and electrodynamics.

Page 23

On the Meaning of the Speed of Light

The velocity of light occupies a unique role in the theory.

Unlike ordinary velocities, it remains invariant for all inertial observers regardless of their motion relative to the source.

This invariance forces the modification of classical ideas concerning space and time.

No signal, force, or material body can propagate faster than light in vacuum.

The speed of light therefore defines the ultimate causal structure of physical reality.

Events connected by signals faster than light would violate the consistency of temporal order between observers.

Thus the constancy of light speed preserves causal coherence throughout physics.

Page 24

Geometry and Physics

Classical geometry assumed that lengths and durations possessed universal meaning independent of motion.

Relativity reveals instead that geometry and measurement procedures are physically conditioned.

Rigid bodies cannot be treated as absolutely invariant objects at relativistic velocities.

Measurements performed in moving systems depend upon synchronization procedures involving light signals.

Thus geometry itself becomes connected to physical operations and observational definitions.

This insight later became one of the foundations of modern spacetime physics.

Page 25

Electromagnetic Unity

One of the deepest achievements of the theory is the unification of electric and magnetic phenomena.

What appears as a purely electric field in one frame may appear as a combination of electric and magnetic fields in another.

The distinction between electricity and magnetism is therefore observer-dependent.

Electromagnetism must be understood as a unified relativistic field.

This insight later became central to twentieth-century theoretical physics.

The asymmetry between moving magnets and moving conductors disappears completely within the relativistic framework.

Page 26

Relativistic Mechanics

Newtonian mechanics remains an excellent approximation when velocities are small compared with the speed of light.

However, at high velocities classical mechanics fails.

Relativistic dynamics predicts that inertia increases with velocity.

The kinetic energy required to continue accelerating a body grows rapidly near light speed.

Infinite energy would be required to accelerate matter to the speed of light itself.

Thus the speed of light forms an unattainable limit for material objects.

Page 27

Philosophical Implications

The theory of relativity profoundly altered humanity’s conception of reality.

Absolute simultaneity disappears.

Observers moving differently through space do not share the same temporal structure.

Time becomes relational rather than universal.

Space and time merge into a connected physical framework later called spacetime.

The observer is no longer external to measurement but becomes part of the physical description itself.

These conceptual changes influenced not only physics but also philosophy, mathematics, and modern intellectual history.

Historical Importance of the 1905 Paper

Einstein’s 1905 paper is regarded as one of the foundational works of modern physics.

It established special relativity and transformed:

  • mechanics
  • electrodynamics
  • optics
  • the philosophy of space and time

The paper also laid conceptual foundations for:

  • general relativity
  • quantum theory
  • particle physics
  • modern cosmology

Its influence extended far beyond physics into twentieth-century thought as a whole.

On the Structure of Scientific Revolutions

Einstein’s achievement did not merely add new equations to existing science.

It replaced the conceptual framework through which physical reality was understood.

The theory preserved the successful empirical results of classical physics while revealing their deeper relativistic structure.

Classical mechanics survives as a limiting approximation valid for small velocities.

Thus scientific revolutions do not necessarily destroy previous theories completely.

Instead they often reveal the conditions under which older theories remain approximately correct.

Page 30

Legacy

The special theory of relativity became one of the pillars of modern science.

Today it is indispensable in:

  • particle accelerators
  • GPS satellite systems
  • nuclear physics
  • astrophysics
  • cosmology

Experimental confirmations of relativistic predictions have repeatedly verified Einstein’s theory with extraordinary precision.

Time dilation, relativistic momentum, and mass–energy effects are now routinely observed in modern laboratories.

The paper of 1905 remains one of the most influential scientific works ever written.

Page 31

Closing Reflection

“On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies” changed the human understanding of nature at the deepest level.

The theory revealed that:

  • space is not absolute
  • time is not universal
  • simultaneity depends upon motion
  • light speed is invariant
  • electricity and magnetism are unified

The paper opened the path toward modern spacetime physics and permanently transformed twentieth-century science.

More than a technical achievement, relativity represented a new way of thinking about reality itself.

Albert Einstein’s 1905 work remains one of the great intellectual landmarks in human history.
